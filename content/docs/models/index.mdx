A workflow's `model.object_detection` node runs whatever detector its
template names. The platform ships general detectors, and this section is
how you get one trained on what your cameras actually see: the loop from
frames to a model an organization owns, run from the browser, with nothing
to install.

## The loop

1. **Frames in.** Upload images, or pull frames straight out of a device
   recording at an interval. They land in a **dataset** with a class list
   you define.
2. **Labels, mostly by machine.** An **assist** job runs a detector over the
   unlabeled frames and writes its boxes as pre-labels, mapped onto your
   classes. You accept, fix or draw in the **annotator**, one keystroke per
   decision.
3. **A version.** A **version** is a frozen snapshot of the labeled frames
   with a train, validation and test split, checked for the things that make
   a training run meaningless (a class with nothing to validate on).
4. **A training job.** Pick a recipe and a compute target; the platform
   trains, exports the model to the engine's format, checks it, evaluates it
   on the validation split and publishes it to your organization's
   **registry** as `<name>@<version>` with its lineage.
5. **Run it.** Pick the model in a template's detection node and run the
   simulator over the same recording; attach the template to devices; or
   download the two files and run them under `xwe` on a laptop or an edge
   device. A template that names your model resolves it in your
   organization's root before the platform's, wherever it runs.

Everything in the loop is a row you can inspect: the dataset and its items,
the assist and training jobs with their logs and metrics, the model with the
dataset version and job it came from.

<Callout title="Who can do what">
  Members annotate, build versions and download models. Admins and owners
  start and cancel jobs (a job costs money) and archive or delete models.
  Viewers read everything and change nothing.
</Callout>

## Where it stands

This is the first release of the section: one family (object detection), one
recipe (`yolov8`, whose license is printed wherever it is chosen), two compute
targets, and the loop above proven end to end from the browser against a real
device recording. [What this does not cover](/models/not-covered) lists the
edges plainly.
