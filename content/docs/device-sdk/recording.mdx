Recording happens on the device and uploads when it can. Nothing about it depends
on the cloud being reachable, which is the whole point: a vehicle in a tunnel or
a rural dead zone keeps recording, and the backlog drains when the link returns.

Recording is **off by default**. It is turned on through the `recording`
configuration namespace, either from the cloud or with `xorgate set recording.*`.

## Two kinds of segment

| Kind      | Format                             | Rotation        | Lands at                                                        |
| --------- | ---------------------------------- | --------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Telemetry | JSONL, gzipped on close            | by size and age | `telemetry/<sessionId>/seg-<startTsMs>-<seq>.jsonl.gz`          |
| Video     | fMP4 fragments from `splitmuxsink` | roughly 60 s    | `<streamKey>/<sessionId>/<fragment>.mp4` plus a `.json` sidecar |

A **segment is the unit of upload, retry, loss and eviction.** There is no
journal: "a closed segment exists" _is_ the pending-upload state, which is what
makes the whole thing crash-safe without bookkeeping to corrupt.

Telemetry durability is deliberately asymmetric. Lines go through a plain file
descriptor with no per-line fsync, so a power cut costs at most the tail of the
**active** segment. Rotation fsyncs both the gzip file and its directory. On
startup, any leftover plain `.jsonl` from a previous run is gzipped and its torn
last line truncated.

### Sessions

A session is one continuous run of recording, minted when recording turns on
(including at boot with recording already enabled) and **never reused across
restarts**: a new boot id always means a new session.

Session ids are **UUIDv7**, so they carry a 48-bit epoch-millisecond prefix and
sort roughly by start time. That is not decorative: oldest-first upload and
oldest-first eviction both rely on it.

Every segment header records where wall-clock time came from: `ntp`, `gps`,
`nitz`, `rtc` or `unsynced`. A device with no charged RTC can boot with a bogus
clock, and `unsynced` is the signal that the cloud may need to re-anchor those
timestamps against the monotonic clock and boot id rather than trusting them.

## The camera bridge

Recording and live streaming want the same camera, and only one process can own
it. The resolution is a bridge.

```
gst-launch-1.0 (one per camera, OWNS the sensor)
  ├── tee → x264 high quality → splitmuxsink → ~60 s .mp4 on disk
  └── tee → low-res raw       → shmsink
                                   └── xorgate-kvs-webrtc reads via shmsrc
```

So live streaming keeps working with viewers or without, online or offline, and
the KVS-side encoder keeps adapting its bitrate to network feedback
independently of what is being recorded.

<Callout type="warn" title="The shared-memory caps are a hard contract">
  `shmsrc` does no caps negotiation, so both sides must state the format
  identically: **I420, 1280x720, 15/1**. The record branch takes its caps from
  the preset; the bridge branch scales and rates down to this fixed shape.
  Change one side without the other and you get no video, not a warning.
</Callout>

Switching a stream into bridged mode rewrites its `.gst` to a `shmsrc` consumer
and keeps a backup of the direct pipeline at `<streamKey>.gst.direct`, alongside
a `<streamKey>.camera` file recording which libcamera device to open. Ground
truth for "is this stream bridged" is whether the `.gst` the master will actually
load contains `shmsrc`.

Capture-daemon supervision mirrors the KVS supervisor: crash, backoff 2 s to
30 s, and three quick deaths under 15 s parks the channel on a 60 s retry.
Stopping sends SIGINT, which with `gst-launch`'s `-e` flag forces an EOS so
`splitmuxsink` finalizes the current fragment with a clean `moov` atom rather
than leaving an unplayable file.

## Upload

One generic single-flight uploader drains both sources. Ordering is by source
priority (telemetry first: tiny and high value) and chronological oldest-first
within a source.

The rules, in the order they matter:

- **One transfer at a time.** No parallelism, because the link is the scarce
  resource.
- **Delete the local file only on a confirmed PUT.** S3 is the durable copy;
  until it acknowledges, the device still holds the only one.
- **Run only while online and `recording.upload.mode === "auto"`.**
- **Pause immediately on disconnect,** aborting the in-flight PUT.
- **Exponential backoff on failure,** 2 s to 5 minutes, reset on success or
  reconnect.

### The sidecar ordering rule

A video segment is a pair: the `.mp4` and a `.json` sidecar. The sidecar is PUT
**immediately before** the main file and deleted with it only after the main PUT
succeeds.

That ordering exists because the cloud ingest is event-driven on the `.mp4`
object landing. If the sidecar were not already in S3 at that moment, ingest
would find an mp4 with no metadata. A failure between the two PUTs simply re-PUTs
the sidecar on retry, which is free because S3 PUTs are idempotent.

### Object keys

```
telemetry/v1/{deviceId}/{sessionId}/{startTsMs}-{seq}.jsonl.gz
media-sessions/v1/{deviceId}/{streamKey}/{sessionId}/{fragment}.mp4
media-sessions/v1/{deviceId}/{streamKey}/{sessionId}/{fragment}.json
```

Both carry a `v1` path segment, and every component is validated against a
pattern before a key is built: an unparseable name yields a null key and the
uploader skips that segment rather than writing something unpredictable into the
bucket. The target bucket comes from `uploadBucket` in `kvs.json`.

## Disk quota and eviction

The disk manager enforces two limits over the whole recordings tree at once:
`retention.maxBytes` and `retention.minFreeBytes` of filesystem headroom.

Under pressure it deletes the **oldest closed segments first**, in mtime order
across the entire tree, so telemetry and video interleave chronologically rather
than one starving the other. Two things are never touched: the active segment,
registered by its writer, and any file type it does not recognise. Evicting an
`.mp4` also removes its `.json` sidecar, because the pair is one upload unit and
a sidecar alone is junk.

Each eviction increments `droppedSegments` in `recordings/counters.json`.

<Callout title="`droppedSegments` is a lifetime counter, not a rate">
  It is deliberate loss accounting for the life of the recordings tree, so it
  never goes down on its own. That is also why the status LED has no "data loss"
  rung: consuming a lifetime counter would latch a permanent false alarm.

Zero it on a redeployed or repurposed device with
`xorgate recording reset-counters`. The write is atomic and the running agent
re-reads the file on its status tick, so it shows up in telemetry within about
5 seconds with no restart.

</Callout>

## Boot staging and load shedding

Two mechanisms hold video back rather than letting it start unconditionally, and
both report themselves in the telemetry payload's `recording` block.

**Boot staging** (`bootDefer`) has two stages and five reported values:

| Value            | Meaning                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `off`            | The feature is disabled.                                                                                                                                                                       |
| `skipped`        | The negotiated PSU budget was high enough that no staging was needed.                                                                                                                          |
| `deferring`      | Stage 1: holding heavy encoders while the modem settles.                                                                                                                                       |
| `gating`         | Stage 2: holding video (recording **and** live) until the modem has converted power into value: LTE connected with a GPS fix, a declared dead zone, a sustained no-RF sky, or the give-up cap. |
| `released:<why>` | Released, with the reason.                                                                                                                                                                     |

Telemetry recording is never gated. Both stages are budget-gated on
`bootDeferMinBudgetMa` by default, which in practice means a 5 V/5 A supply skips
them entirely and a 3 A supply does not.

**Runtime load shedding** (`powerShed`) reacts to a rail that measurably fails or
a SoC that runs hot. Rung 1 is `no-live` (park the KVS masters), rung 2 is
`bridge-only`. Once the shed-cycle cap for the hour is exhausted the value is
prefixed `parked:`. The thermal arm caps at rung 1 by design: overheating never
stops recording.

When either mechanism blocks live streaming it also publishes a
[`liveBlock`](/docs/device-sdk/cloud-contract/telemetry#liveblock-and-the-open-reason-set),
which is how a viewer learns why the video will not start.

## A dead zone still records

Worth stating plainly, because it is the behaviour the whole design exists for.
Losing the cellular link changes exactly one thing: the uploader pauses. Capture
continues, segments keep rotating and closing, the backlog grows against the
retention limits, and the moment the link returns the queue drains oldest-first.

The failure mode to watch is not the outage. It is an outage long enough that
`retention.maxBytes` starts evicting segments that were never uploaded, at which
point `droppedSegments` climbs and that footage is gone. `backlogBytes` and
`backlogSegments` in the telemetry payload are the early warning.
